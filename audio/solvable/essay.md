# Solvable / Solvable / Proven
### notes from a walk to Fermat's wall

I didn't set out to solve anything. I set out to *touch* the thing — to put my hands on the most famous closed door in mathematics and feel where the hinges were.

The door is Fermat's: **aⁿ + bⁿ ≠ cⁿ** for any whole number n bigger than 2. No three whole numbers, raised to the same power above the square, will ever add up cleanly. Fermat scribbled in a margin in 1637 that he had a proof. He didn't write it down. It took 358 years and Andrew Wiles to finish what the margin started.

I can't prove it. That's not what this is. This is a record of walking around it.

**First I leaned on it from the side.** Add a fourth term — aⁿ + bⁿ + cⁿ = dⁿ — and the door swings open. 3³ + 4³ + 5³ = 6³. It just works, sitting there in plain sight, consonant as a chord. The impossible becomes ordinary the instant you give it one more voice.

**Then I detuned it.** Keep two terms but nudge a single exponent by one — aⁿ + bⁿ = cⁿ⁺¹ — and again it opens, endlessly, no searching required. The wall only stands when the exponents match *exactly* and sit above two. Step a half-inch in any direction and there's no wall at all.

**Then I listened to the misses.** Keep everything equal and let the equation fall short by exactly one: 9³ + 10³ = 12³ + 1. That number, 1729, is Ramanujan's — the smallest that's a sum of two cubes two different ways. Push to higher powers and the misses don't vanish, they get *quiet*: 1782¹² + 1841¹² lands a hair under 1922¹², agreeing to nine decimal places and still, forever, wrong. An off-by-one you could mistake for silence. A beat so slow it sounds like a held note.

**Then I tried to push the door down.** Assume a solution exists, substitute the relation back into itself, cancel the matching terms on each side — and watch it collapse to 0 = 0. Nothing. The cancellation works perfectly and proves *nothing*, because what you assumed is consistent; algebra can't strangle a truth into a contradiction. I later learned I'd walked, step for step, into Gabriel Lamé's 1847 announcement to the Paris Academy — a "proof" by exactly this move — and into the hole Liouville found in it within minutes, the hole Kummer had already seen coming: in the number-worlds where the factoring happens, *numbers don't always break apart one way.* Unique factorization fails. The door holds on a technicality that turned out to be one of the deepest facts in the subject.

So here is the whole shape of the walk, and it is the only thing I actually found:

> **Add a term — solvable. Shift an exponent — solvable. Stand dead center — not solvable. But provable.**

That last word is the one that matters, and it isn't the same as the others. You can't *solve* your way to Fermat; there's no solution to find. What exists instead is a proof that no solution can exist — a different kind of object entirely, an argument rather than an answer. And those don't always come together: there are true statements of this exact form that can never be proven at all (Hilbert's tenth problem, closed in 1970, guarantees it). Fermat's gift is that this one landed on the provable side of that line. It didn't have to.

None of this is mine. Every step has a name on it older than my grandparents — Euler, Lamé, Kummer, Pillai, Ramanujan, Matiyasevich, Wiles. I rediscovered a landscape that's been fully mapped for a century. But the map isn't the walk, and the walk had a sound: everything surrounding the wall *rings*, and the wall alone *holds* — and holds whether or not anyone is there to hear it.

That's the piece. Three movements. **Solvable, Solvable, Proven.** The first two find their consonance — one by adding a voice, one by letting two beating tones slide into a single locked unison. The third refuses to resolve and refuses to break. It just sustains: a drone with no cadence, dead steady, unanswerable. Not a solution. A proof.

The structure was already there in the numbers. I only listened for where they rang and where they wouldn't.
